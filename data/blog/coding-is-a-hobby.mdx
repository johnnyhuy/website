My software career started as a hobby. Building websites for gaming communities on Steam. Tinkering with whatever was broken. Nobody called it engineering.

Somewhere along the way it became a profession with a craft, a ladder, and specialties you could spend a decade inside. Cloud. Backend. The deep stuff. I loved that part.

Now parts of the job look like a hobby again. Coding is a hobby. I don't do that any more.

## The Five Stages

Every engineer I know is somewhere on a grief curve about AI right now. The framing that lands is the oldest one we have: denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance.[^1]

Denial is "these tools are crap" or "it's all overhyped." Anger is the thread where anyone excited about agents gets dunked on. Bargaining is "AI can do some things, just not my job." Depression is the identity crisis, realising the craft that defined you is being automated. Acceptance is the only stage where any useful work happens.

I've watched teams sit in each one. The most common stall is bargaining. "I just want it to generate unit tests." "I don't trust it." "I don't want my day job to be prompting." That last one is wild to me. Prompting is the new typing. Complaining about it is like complaining that your editor has autocomplete.

Getting to acceptance six months before your peers is the only durable edge going.

## The Ego Conversation

There's a fight happening about whether AI is shattering developer egos. It isn't. Inflated egos exist in every industry and AI amplifies whatever is already there.[^2]

The more useful read is the boring one. If you were good at building things, AI makes you better. If you were average, it lifts you too. The gap between good and great probably widens, because strong tool use compounds skill.

So the grief isn't really about ego. It's about the specific thing you were proud of being fast at. Typing out a clean abstraction. Knowing the API by heart. Shipping a feature before lunch. Those skills still matter. They just aren't the bottleneck any more.

What transfers is problem decomposition, systems thinking, and judgement on trade-offs. What dies is memorising APIs and typing speed.

## The Jobs Don't Have Names Yet

The fear under the grief is that there's nothing to move into. It's worth taking seriously and it's worth remembering how bad we are at this particular prediction.

Ask someone in 1800 what work their descendants would do in two hundred years and nobody says "airline pilot." Not because the job was hidden. Because it couldn't be imagined without the thing that made it possible.

Same shape now. The roles showing up at conferences this year - forward deployed, product engineering, agent orchestration, evaluation, the person who owns the verification scaffolding - didn't exist as titles eighteen months ago. Some of them will look quaint in five years. Some of them will be departments.

The honest position is that I can't name the job I'll be doing in a decade either. What I can do is be useful in the transition, which is a much more tractable goal.

## Back to the Hobby

Here's the part I didn't expect.

The work I do now looks a lot like the work I did at fifteen. Making things because something was missing. Wiring a stack together out of whatever free tiers were around. Shipping before it was ready and fixing it in public. Tinkering.

The difference is scale. Then it was a forum skin and a server list. Now it's a product with a pricing page and a real customer. The posture is identical. Curiosity, a laptop, and the willingness to look stupid for a week.

Nobody at fifteen told me I was doing knowledge work. I was just building things. It took a decade inside org structures to learn that the thing I'd been doing for fun was the thing the industry would eventually be rebuilt around.

## The Generalist Trap

Being a generalist is great for expanding your knowledge and you will absolutely miss the parts that used to be a specialty.

I spent years going deep on cloud and backend work. Real depth. The kind where you can reason about a failure mode before it happens because you've been paged by it at 2am. That knowledge doesn't become worthless. It becomes background.

There's a shape I keep coming back to. The old advice was to be T-shaped - broad across many things, deep in one. I reckon the shape that survives now is M-shaped. Broad, and deep in a couple of places at once, and able to go deep again on short notice when a task calls for it.

The reason is practical. A generalist who can't go deep gets stuck at the surface of every problem. A specialist who can't go broad gets stuck when the problem moves. The people who thrived at the AI Engineer World's Fair this year were the ones moving into product engineering and forward-deployed roles, where the job is articulating the idea to a room as much as building it.

Deep specialists used to be able to stay in their lane for a career. That lane is getting narrower.

## What Actually Helps

Three things, and none of them are about typing.

Get good at decomposing a problem into something a fleet of agents can execute in parallel. That's the core skill and almost nobody has it yet.

Get good at verification. The bottleneck isn't generation, it's knowing whether what came back is right.

Get good at deciding what not to build. Attention is the scarce resource now. Not tokens, not compute.

One last thing about staying sane. You do not need every new model. You need to know the categories exist, follow a couple of people you trust, and build something real most weeks. The meta-skill is learning how to learn the workflow, because the workflow changes monthly. Tutorial-grinding is procrastination with extra tabs.

And build the thing. Not a tutorial-grade replica. The thing you've been circling for a year. Watching someone else's workflow won't teach you yours.
